# Historical Research and Story Concepts

> Research pass, 2026-09-08. Two things live here: a reading of the period the game sits in,
> and **30 story concepts** built on it. Nothing is decided. `docs/STORY.md` holds the current
> draft; when a concept below is chosen, that file is rewritten from it.
>
> Sources are listed at the end. Where a popular image turned out to be later invention rather
> than history, it is marked — those are the places a game usually gets caught.

---

# Part 1 — The summary

**Who we are.** Not samurai. A **samurai** is a retainer: he has a lord, a stipend, a name on a
register. A **ronin** is a warrior without one — the word means "wave man", a man who drifts.
Not a rank, a condition, and in this period an enormous one: roughly **400,000** at the time of
Sekigahara (1600), a hundred thousand of them fighting at Osaka in 1614, and as many as **half a
million** drifting the country under the third shogun. Peace did not absorb them; peace is what
created them.

**Why they existed in that number.** The shogunate destroyed houses on purpose. **Kaieki** —
attainder — confiscated a lord's domain for an infraction, and every retainer of that house fell
out of employment with him. Fukushima Masanori lost a 490,000-koku domain in 1619 for repairing
his own castle without permission. Over the period more than 600 domains were recorded and never
more than about 270 existed at once. Each closure produced men.

**What was done to them.** They were banished from cities, restricted to certain quarters, and
for a time **forbidden to take a new master**. That is a closed loop: no lord, no stipend, no
permission to get one. It ended in the **Keian Uprising** of 1651, a rōnin plot to burn Edo and
storm the castle; it was betrayed, and thirty-three conspirators were executed along with male
relatives.

**Bushido is a trap.** The code as it is popularly imagined was written down *after* the wars —
Yamaga Sokō (1622-1685) codified it, *Hagakure* was composed 1710-1716 during a century of peace,
and the modern global image comes largely from Nitobe Inazō's English book of **1900**. Before
that, warrior ethics were neither uniform nor codified. A game set in the 1630s should not run on
"the bushido code". It should run on **name** — reputation as the only asset a masterless man
holds, which is exactly what the honour system already models.

**The moral line is in the statute book.** This is the useful discovery. A 1616 decree capped
**nenki hōkō** — fixed-term service contracts — at **three years**, and prescribed **death** for
traffickers selling people into perpetual bondage; a 1619 edict added death for kidnappers, and
freed anyone held four years or more. Separately, **tsujigiri** — cutting down a stranger at a
crossroads to test a new blade or technique — was made a **capital offence in 1602**, and was
already regarded as cowardice rather than warrior practice. So a school that buys people on
three-year contracts is inside the law; a school that buys them forever, or trains on living
people, is committing capital crimes. Grey and black are not invented for the game — they are
the difference between two clauses.

**What sits at the edges** (each expanded in Part 2): the gaudy violent gangs of early Edo; the
hereditary sword-testers who cut corpses and sold pills made from them; the blind guild that
became the country's licensed moneylender; the outcast castes who guarded executions and buried
the dead; the registered vendetta, which made revenge a licensed act; the unregistered poor
rounded up and sent to drain a gold mine; the inquisition that paid informants; and the year the
sea closed and Japanese abroad were forbidden, on pain of death, to come home.

# Part 2 — The detail

## 2.1 The ronin condition

Numbers first, because they set the tone. ~400,000 masterless men around 1600; ~100,000 at Osaka;
up to 500,000 drifting under Iemitsu (r. 1623-1651). The state's answer was containment: expulsion
from cities, restricted quarters, and a period in which taking a new master was prohibited
outright. A man in that position kept two things — the right to wear the swords, and his name.

**For the game:** this is why a dojo can buy fighters at all, why they are desperate, why
permadeath bites (the dead leave nothing behind but a name), and why honour is the currency. It
also gives the roster its texture: your people are not adventurers, they are surplus.

## 2.2 Kaieki — the state as the manufacturer of enemies

Attainder came in three grades: income reduction (*genpō*), forced transfer (*tenpō*), and
outright abolition of the house (*kaieki*). Pretexts could be thin — Fukushima Masanori's was
unauthorised castle repair; the shogunate's own senior men reportedly hesitated, fearing it would
provoke the other lords. His retainers later cremated his body before the inspectors arrived, and
the house was punished again for it.

**For the game:** the cleanest origin story for a master. He was somebody's retainer; the house
was taken apart on a technicality; the man who wrote the report still holds office. That is an
antagonist with paperwork instead of fangs.

## 2.3 The Keian plot, 1651

Planned from 1645 by Yui Shōsetsu and Marubashi Chūya with a small group of rōnin and their
students: fire Edo, storm the castle in the confusion, seize Sunpu, move on Osaka and Kyoto,
timed to the death of Iemitsu and the succession of a child shogun. Marubashi fell ill and talked
in his delirium; thirty-three were arrested, interrogated and executed. Yui killed himself. Then
the state relaxed — inheritance rules for lords were loosened, and rōnin were permitted to take
new masters.

**For the game:** the plot is a perfect background pressure. Your school is full of exactly the
men the authorities fear. Any gathering of armed masterless men is a conspiracy until proven
otherwise — which gives the state a reason to watch you that has nothing to do with the villain.

## 2.4 Service, bondage, and the three-year cap

The 1616 decree: *nenki hōkō* contracts capped at three years; capital punishment for traffickers
selling into perpetual bondage; existing perpetual agreements annulled; victims allowed to return
to their families. The 1619 Jōjō edict: death for kidnappers, fines or imprisonment for
intermediaries, freedom for those bound four years or more.

**For the game:** this is the recruit market, and it is the moral spine. Paying off a person's
debt and taking three years of their service is lawful. It is also, plainly, buying a person to
spend in fights you profit from. The game does not need to editorialise; it only needs to let the
player notice.

## 2.5 Tsujigiri and the sword-testers

*Tsujigiri* — "crossroads cutting" — was testing a blade or a new technique on a random passer-by,
usually at night, usually a merchant or a peasant. Made a capital offence in 1602. Contemporary
opinion held it cowardly and associated it with bandits, not with respectable practice.

The legal alternative was **tameshigiri** on corpses. The **Yamada Asaemon** line held the
hereditary office of official sword tester (*otameshi goyō*) — appraising blades by cutting the
bodies of executed criminals at the execution grounds, performing decapitations, and issuing
formal certificates of a blade's quality. Their real income was the bodies themselves: unclaimed
corpses were handed over, and organs were rendered into pills — "Yamada pills", sold across the
country.

**For the game:** an antagonist that is respectable, licensed, and quietly monstrous. A house
whose business is bodies has a structural interest in a province where men die in duels. It can
be your buyer.

## 2.6 The gaudy gangs

**Kabukimono** — "the deviants" — appeared as the Sengoku wars ended: rōnin, drifting warriors,
and former servants of samurai houses, formed into street gangs. They dressed to be seen: garish
kimono with lead-weighted hems, velvet lapels, foreign cloth, animal skins, women's clothing,
long or extravagantly dressed hair instead of the standard topknot, and swords decorated to
excess with exaggerated blade lengths. They ate without paying, robbed townsfolk, fought each
other after dark, and cut people down to try a new sword. Their peak was 1596-1615, and the
shogunate's tightening ended them.

Against them stood the **machi-yakko**, townsmen's self-defence bands. **Banzuiin Chōbē** — a
real Edo figure, later heavily fictionalised — led one in Asakusa and ran an **employment agency
for rōnin**.

**For the game:** the most visually distinctive enemy available, and historically the correct
one for a fight-school setting. Also note Chōbē's agency: a business that places masterless men
into work is one door away from what the player's dojo is.

## 2.7 Schools, licences, and challenges

Kenjutsu ran on *ryūha* — discrete schools with their own curriculum and lineage. Progress was
sold: recorded Edo fees run 5 *monme* of silver for the first level, 10 for the middle, and
**1 *ryō* of gold for full transmission** (*menkyo kaiden*) — roughly ¥100,000 in modern terms
for the top certificate. The **Yagyū Shinkage-ryū** became the shogunate's official school under
**Yagyū Munenori**, who was instructor to the second and third shoguns and, from 1632, the first
**ōmetsuke** — the inspector general who oversaw surveillance of the lords and the bakufu's
intelligence apparatus.

Inter-school matches (*taryū jiai*) were real, and *musha shugyō* — a warrior's wandering
pilgrimage from province to province to test himself against schools — was a real practice.
**But:** the cinematic *dōjō yaburi*, storming a rival school and smashing its signboard, is
largely a product of later literature, theatre and film. Build on the match, not the raid.

**For the game:** the licence is money and the licence is politics — the same family that teaches
the shogun's sword runs his spies. A school that refuses the official curriculum is not merely
unfashionable; it is unregistered.

## 2.8 Revenge as a licensed act

**Katakiuchi** (*adauchi*) — blood revenge — was legal, and regulated. From the Edo period an
avenger had to **apply for permission**: a samurai took a permit from his lord, and if the pursuit
crossed into another province he reported to a magistrate so the case could be entered on the
**vendetta register**. It covered the killing of a direct senior relative — a parent or elder
brother — and not a wife, child or younger sibling. It was abolished in 1873.

**For the game:** revenge with paperwork. A registered vendetta is a licence to kill a specific
named man, and it can be filed, refused, delayed, or filed *against you*. This is possibly the
single most game-shaped fact in the whole research pass.

## 2.9 The unregistered, and the mine

*Mushukunin* — people struck from the household registers, the homeless and the displaced — were
rounded up in Edo, Osaka and Nagasaki under public-order measures and sent to the **Sado gold
mine**, roughly 1,800 of them, to work the drainage pumps on ten-year terms. The work was
notoriously short-lived. Many were refugees pushed off the land by famine and disaster.

**For the game:** a fate worse than the dojo, which is what the dojo needs to exist against. A
man taken off your roster does not simply leave; there is somewhere for him to go, and it is the
pumps.

## 2.10 Outcasts

Two hereditary outcast categories: **eta**, whose work was skinning and tanning — polluting under
Buddhist and Shinto convention — and **hinin**, "non-persons", pressed into begging, street
performance, guarding execution grounds, executions themselves and burying the executed. They
lived in segregated enclaves. Hinin status was hereditary, but unlike eta, a hinin's children
could return to commoner status under certain conditions.

**For the game:** a source of fighters with a specific, legible reason to gamble their lives —
status is recoverable, and the dojo is a route. Also a natural place for the school's undertaker,
and for the man who deals with the enemy's corpses.

## 2.11 The inquisition and the informant economy

Anti-Christian surveillance was systematised as **shūmon aratame** (from 1640). Everyone had to
obtain temple registration; village officials compiled registers of religious affiliation.
**Fumi-e** — treading on an image of Christ or Mary to prove one was not a Christian — was used
from 1629 in Nagasaki, with the ceremony held annually before the magistrate, in temples for
commoners and in fortresses for samurai. Crucially, **informants were paid**; notices offering
rewards persisted into the Meiji period. Hidden Christians (*kakure kirishitan*) trod the image
and kept the faith secretly for two and a half centuries.

**For the game:** a mechanic for suspicion. Anyone can be denounced, and denunciation pays. A
roster is a list of people whose pasts you did not check.

## 2.12 Shimabara, 1637-38

A rising on the Shimabara peninsula and the Amakusa islands against a lord's taxation and abuses:
around 40,000 people, mostly Christian peasants, joined by fishermen, craftsmen, merchants and
**rōnin**, under a charismatic sixteen-year-old, Amakusa Shirō. The shogunate sent over 125,000
troops, assisted by Dutch naval gunnery, and took the rebel stronghold at Hara Castle after a
siege. Shirō and an estimated **37,000** rebels and sympathisers were killed. It effectively
ended open Christianity in Japan and hardened the drive toward isolation.

**For the game:** the last time masterless men fought a war, they lost it with 37,000 dead. If
the master is a survivor, everything about his refusal to raise an army makes sense.

## 2.13 The sea closes

Between 1600 and 1635 more than 350 Japanese ships sailed abroad under the **red seal** permit
system. The **1635 edict** ended it: Japanese were forbidden to travel overseas, and **any
Japanese living abroad who attempted to return was to be put to death**. The Portuguese were
expelled in 1639 after Shimabara; the Dutch were confined to Dejima in 1641.

Abroad, there had been real communities. **Ban Yipun**, the Japanese quarter of Ayutthaya in
Siam, held around a thousand Japanese; **Yamada Nagamasa**, a palanquin-bearer's son, rose to
govern a Siamese province before his death in 1630, after which the settlement was attacked and
destroyed, survivors fleeing to Cambodia.

**For the game:** an expiry date on an entire category of person. A returnee is a walking capital
offence, and a school that shelters one is complicit.

## 2.14 The blind guild

The **Tōdōza** was the officially chartered guild of blind men, patronised by the Muromachi and
then the Edo shogunate, abolished only in 1871. It was ranked — four ranks, sixteen grades,
seventy-three steps to the top — and held effective monopolies on performing the *Tale of the
Heike*, on massage and acupuncture, and, increasingly, on **money-lending at high interest**,
formally approved so that members could buy advancement through the ranks. Blind men were
exempted from the outcast classification that caught comparable street professions.

**For the game:** a creditor that is lawful, protected, patient and impossible to intimidate.
The best kind of pressure: it never breaks a rule.

## 2.15 The Akō incident — how a real vendetta actually went

1701: Asano attacked the official Kira inside the shogun's palace, wounding him. For the breach
Asano was ordered to commit seppuku the same day and his domain was confiscated — his retainers
became rōnin. In 1703, forty-seven of them killed Kira, carried his head to their lord's grave,
and turned themselves in. The authorities deliberated **six weeks**, then ordered all of them to
commit seppuku: the loyalty was acknowledged, the law was not bent. Public sympathy made them
heroes, and the theatre made them permanent.

**For the game:** the model to learn from. The state does not need to be evil to be the thing
that kills you. And the six weeks of deliberation is a better dramatic clock than any invented one.

# Part 3 — Thirty story concepts

Each is judged against the three structural tests the game imposes:

1. **The clock** — why exactly 180 days, and why can't you act sooner?
2. **The repetition** — where do a season's worth of separate, escalating fights come from?
3. **The last night** — why five fights, in one night, with no recovery between them?

`Cost` says how much of the built game would have to change. `Grey/Black` names the moral line.

---

### 1. Three Years and a Name
Your master ran a school on lawful three-year service contracts: pay a man's debt, take three
years, and he leaves with a name of his own. He was killed for it, and the contracts he held are
now claimable by his creditors.
**Grey/Black:** you buy people lawfully and spend them in lethal fights · they buy people
permanently and train on the living.
**Clock:** an expulsion order clears unrecognised schools at the end of the season; unclaimed
contracts pass to the buyer with the deepest purse — them.
**Repetition:** every contract fight is money toward buying the remaining terms outright.
**Last night:** an official inter-school match, five men, before a magistrate, decides which
school inherits.
**History:** 1616 three-year cap; 1602 tsujigiri ban; rōnin expulsion from cities.
**Cost:** none. The market, honour and permadeath already model it.

### 2. The Testers
A hereditary house holds the office of official sword tester: it cuts corpses, certifies blades,
and sells medicine rendered from bodies. Demand outgrew supply. Now it pays for duels, sponsors
schools, and is quietly moving from the dead to the nearly dead.
**Grey/Black:** you sell your dead to them to fund the school · they arrange the deaths.
**Clock:** the year's certification is issued at season's end; the house that supplies the bodies
writes it, and a school it names as unfit is finished.
**Repetition:** every duel is a body, and the body has a price on the sheet.
**Last night:** five blades to be certified, five men to test them on, in one sitting.
**History:** the Yamada Asaemon line, *otameshi goyō*, corpse pills.
**Cost:** low. Add a corpse-sale line to the economy — which also fixes "an expedition is pure loss".

### 3. The Register
Informing pays. Anyone can denounce anyone as a hidden Christian, and the reward is public money.
Your master was denounced and did not survive the interrogation. Half your roster has a past that
will not survive an inquiry.
**Grey/Black:** you falsify registrations to protect your people · the enemy manufactures
denunciations as a business.
**Clock:** the annual inquisition ceremony, held in the first days of the year, before the
magistrate; everyone on your books must appear.
**Repetition:** each victory raises your visibility, and visibility invites inquiry.
**Last night:** five of your people are called to tread the image in public. Each refusal is a
fight.
**History:** *shūmon aratame* from 1640, *fumi-e* from 1629, paid informants.
**Cost:** medium. Needs a per-warrior "past" and a suspicion track.

### 4. The Unregistered
Men struck from the household registers are rounded up and sent to drain a gold mine on ten-year
terms nobody survives. Your school is where such men are not asked for papers. The contractor is
paid by the head.
**Grey/Black:** you hide men from the state and profit from their fighting · he sells men into a
mine to die.
**Clock:** the season's roundup quota is filled by the last day; whoever is short makes it up
from the nearest unregistered roster — yours.
**Repetition:** each fight buys a registration for one man, one name at a time.
**Last night:** five of the contractor's enforcers, at the pit head, before the boats leave.
**History:** *mushukunin* roundups in Edo, Osaka and Nagasaki; ~1,800 sent to Sado.
**Cost:** low.

### 5. A Licence to Kill
Revenge is legal here if it is registered: file the petition, name the man, and the killing is
lawful. A house has learned to farm the register — forged grievances, hired avengers, deaths that
are entirely legal. Your master's name is on one of their filings, posthumously.
**Grey/Black:** you take registered vendettas for money · they manufacture the grievances.
**Clock:** a filed vendetta expires; yours runs the length of the season.
**Repetition:** the man you want is protected by a chain of lawful avengers, each of whom must
be met before you reach him.
**Last night:** five defenders standing between you and the name on your own petition.
**History:** *katakiuchi* permits, the magistrate's vendetta register, the direct-senior-relative rule.
**Cost:** low, and it converts the bounty system into a story system.

### 6. The Deviants
Gangs of ex-retainers in lead-hemmed kimono, foreign cloth and absurdly long swords own the night
in this town. They kill for the pleasure of trying a blade. Their patron is a bannerman, and the
magistrate will not touch him.
**Grey/Black:** you become the town's private muscle and take its money · they are simply
predators with protection.
**Clock:** the patron's posting ends with the season; while he holds office nothing can be
charged against them.
**Repetition:** the gangs are many and rivalrous — you can turn them on each other.
**Last night:** the patron's five retained swordsmen, on the last night of his office.
**History:** kabukimono; machi-yakko; Banzuiin Chōbē's band in Asakusa.
**Cost:** none. It is the most visually distinct option — the enemy is *recognisable at a glance*.

### 7. The Agency
Your master ran a placement house: he found work for masterless men. It was the only honest one.
A rival broker places men where nobody asks them to come back — mine gangs, guard details on
roads that are ambushes, duels arranged as entertainment.
**Grey/Black:** you also place men in danger, but you count them back · he counts the fee.
**Clock:** the licence to broker is granted yearly.
**Repetition:** every placement is a job with a fight at the end of it.
**Last night:** five placements in one night — the rival's standing offer, taken all at once.
**History:** Chōbē's employment agency for rōnin; the ban on rōnin taking new masters.
**Cost:** low. It reframes the contract queue rather than replacing it.

### 8. The Report
A house was destroyed on a technicality — a castle wall repaired without permission — and every
retainer of it fell into the street. Your master was one. The inspector who wrote the report has
been promoted twice since. Your school is made of that house's survivors.
**Grey/Black:** you are rebuilding a private army under another name · he is a functionary who
did his job well.
**Clock:** an appeal against attainder may be heard when the inspector's circuit returns, once a
year; the school must be creditable enough to be heard.
**Repetition:** creditability is built one sanctioned fight at a time.
**Last night:** the inspector's escort — five men — on the road, on the night he arrives.
**History:** *kaieki*; Fukushima Masanori's 490,000-koku domain lost for unauthorised repairs, 1619.
**Cost:** low. Note the villain is never personally evil, which makes him harder to write and
better to beat.

### 9. The Shadow of the Great Inspector
The man who teaches the shogun's own sword also runs his spies. Schools that accept his licence
receive work; the work is killing, deniably. Your master refused and was buried as a suicide.
**Grey/Black:** you take the work you can stomach to survive · they use the sword arts as an
intelligence service.
**Clock:** the licences are reissued annually; refuse twice and the school is struck off.
**Repetition:** the deniable work is endless, and each job compromises you further.
**Last night:** five of his instructors, in a closed hall, with no witnesses to record it.
**History:** Yagyū Munenori, sword instructor to two shoguns, first *ōmetsuke* from 1632.
**Cost:** low-medium; the honour system carries the compromise nicely.

### 10. The Certificate
Full transmission costs a gold piece and is worth more than the money — it is the right to teach.
A cartel forges them. Provincial towns are full of "masters" who cannot fight, and their students
die on the roads. Your master proved it, and was killed by a man whose certificate he had exposed.
**Grey/Black:** you teach without any certificate at all, which is also illegal · they sell
lethal fictions.
**Clock:** the province's schools are examined and re-listed once a year.
**Repetition:** each false master must be beaten publicly for the forgery to be visible.
**Last night:** the cartel's own five, who are genuinely good — the forgery funded real training.
**History:** *menkyo kaiden* fees (5 monme / 10 monme / 1 ryō); *taryū jiai*.
**Cost:** none.

### 11. Return Forbidden
The sea is closed. Japanese abroad may not come home; if they do, the penalty is death. Your
school is full of men who came home anyway — from the trading ports, the mercenary companies, the
quarter in Siam. Someone is selling their names.
**Grey/Black:** you shelter capital offenders and use them as fighters · the informer trades in
men who cannot go to the magistrate.
**Clock:** the port registry is reconciled with the temple registers at year's end; every
unmatched name is investigated.
**Repetition:** each fight buys one forged registration, or silences one seller.
**Last night:** five names, five men come to collect them, at the harbour.
**History:** the 1635 edict — overseas travel banned, returnees to be put to death; red-seal
voyages, 350+ ships 1600-1635.
**Cost:** low. Gives the roster a built-in reason for exotic weapons and foreign styles.

### 12. Ban Yipun
Not in Japan at all. A Japanese quarter of a thousand people in a foreign capital, a decade after
its famous headman died and the settlement was attacked. You run its fighting school. Home is
closed by law — the sea edict means these people can never return. The court wants the quarter's
men as soldiers and its land for someone else.
**Grey/Black:** you rent your men to a foreign court to keep the quarter fed · the court intends
to be rid of you once the fighting is done.
**Clock:** the last ship of the season; the quarter's people leave with you or not at all.
**Repetition:** court service is a fight a week.
**Last night:** five champions of the court, wagered against the quarter's passage.
**History:** Yamada Nagamasa, Ban Yipun in Ayutthaya, ~1,000 Japanese; destroyed after 1630;
survivors fled to Cambodia.
**Cost:** medium — a different setting, new enemy visuals. Also the most original.

### 13. The Guild's Terms
Your master borrowed from the blind guild — the only lawful lender that would touch a school of
masterless men. The guild is chartered by the shogunate, ranked, patient, and never breaks a
rule. The term falls due at the end of the season, and the collateral is the contracts of your
people.
**Grey/Black:** you took money you knew you could not repay, betting other men's lives on it ·
the guild does nothing illegal at all, which is precisely the horror.
**Clock:** the term. Nothing else needs inventing.
**Repetition:** every purse is a payment; the schedule is the game.
**Last night:** the guild permits one settlement of honour: five fights, and the debt is voided
or the school is taken.
**History:** the Tōdōza — shogunal patronage, four ranks and seventy-three steps, licensed
high-interest lending, abolished 1871.
**Cost:** none. It is the debt story with a villain who cannot be stabbed.

### 14. Thirty-Seven Thousand
A rebellion of peasants and masterless men was put down with foreign guns and a siege, and
everyone inside was killed — thirty-seven thousand. Your master survived it on the winning side
and refused an order at the end. Now he is dead and you are training the children of the losers.
**Grey/Black:** you are raising the survivors of a heresy into fighters, which is treason in
embryo · the officer who gave the order is now hunting the remnant for the reward.
**Clock:** the surviving registers are reconciled at the year's inquisition; every name on them
that is still alive is to be accounted for.
**Repetition:** the hunt is province-wide and comes in waves.
**Last night:** five of the officer's hunters at the school gate.
**History:** Shimabara 1637-38; ~40,000 rebels, 125,000 government troops with Dutch naval
support, ~37,000 killed; Amakusa Shirō aged sixteen.
**Cost:** low-medium.

### 15. Six Weeks
Your people did something the whole province approves of and the law cannot permit. They are in
custody, and the authorities are deliberating. The deliberation has no fixed end — but sentiment
moves it, and sentiment is built in public, one sanctioned fight at a time.
**Grey/Black:** you are manufacturing public feeling to bend a court · the official pressing for
execution is entirely correct in law.
**Clock:** the deliberation. Days remaining are days before the judgement is written.
**Repetition:** every public victory is a weight on the scale.
**Last night:** the judgement is read; five of the household's retainers refuse it.
**History:** the Akō incident — Asano's same-day seppuku 1701, the killing of Kira 1703, six
weeks of deliberation, seppuku ordered for all forty-six.
**Cost:** medium — needs a public-sentiment track, which the chat layer could *be*.

### 16. The Status
Your fighters are legally not commoners. Their status is hereditary and their children can climb
out of it — under conditions. The man who administers the enclave rents them out for guard work,
for burials, for the execution ground, and takes the fee.
**Grey/Black:** you buy their contracts to fight, which is another kind of renting · he sells
their status back to them a piece at a time and never runs out of pieces.
**Clock:** the status registers are revised once a year; a name can be moved, once, if it is
worth moving.
**Repetition:** each fight is one name's worth of standing.
**Last night:** five fights for five names, at the enclave's gate.
**History:** eta and hinin; hinin status hereditary but escapable under conditions; duties
included guarding executions and burying the executed.
**Cost:** low. The most emotionally direct version of "three years and a name".

### 17. The Other Side of the Vendetta
Your school is hired to protect a man forty-seven avengers intend to kill. He is unpleasant,
legally in the right, and the province wants him dead. Your master took the contract and was
killed for it; the contract passes to you with the school.
**Grey/Black:** you are defending a man everyone hates, for money, and you are right in law ·
the "villains" are sympathetic and doomed.
**Clock:** the avengers must act within the season or lose their standing to act at all.
**Repetition:** they come in ones and threes for months before they come as a body.
**Last night:** the assault, five waves, one night, no recovery.
**History:** the Akō incident inverted; the vendetta register.
**Cost:** low. Structurally identical to the main design, morally reversed — and it makes the
defence-of-the-dojo mechanic the *centrepiece* instead of a punishment.

### 18. The Pilgrimage, Reversed
Your master's school became famous for the wrong reason: he beat someone he should not have. Now
every wandering swordsman in the country is coming to test himself against his students — and he
is dead, so the students are what is left. You do not choose your fights; they arrive.
**Grey/Black:** you take gate money from challenges you know your weakest man will lose · the
schools sending challengers are farming your reputation deliberately.
**Clock:** the pilgrimage season; when it ends the school's ranking is fixed for good.
**Repetition:** built in — the challengers *are* the content.
**Last night:** five arrive together, sent by one school.
**History:** *musha shugyō*; *taryū jiai*. (Note: the signboard-smashing raid is later fiction.)
**Cost:** low, but it inverts the offer queue — fights come to you.

### 19. The Quarter
Masterless men are confined to one district, forbidden to take new masters, and the warden runs
the district as a private fief: he licenses the fights, takes a cut, and decides who eats.
**Grey/Black:** you rise inside his system and become part of it · he keeps five hundred armed
men penned and profitable.
**Clock:** the district is to be cleared and rebuilt at season's end; everyone in it is dispersed.
**Repetition:** the licensed fights are the district's whole economy.
**Last night:** the warden's five, in the district, on the night before the clearance.
**History:** rōnin banished from cities, restricted to quarters, barred from new masters.
**Cost:** none. It also explains why every fight is nearby — a virtue for a game with no map.

### 20. The Ranking
A blade's worth is set by test results, and the tests are written by men who can be bought. A
cartel of smiths and testers has been fixing the rankings — and the tests that matter most are
performed on the living, in fights they arrange. Your school has been someone's proving ground
for years without knowing it.
**Grey/Black:** you keep fighting for them once you know, because the purses are the best in the
province · they are pricing steel in men.
**Clock:** the ranking list is published annually.
**Repetition:** each arranged fight is a test of a specific blade — and the enemy's equipment is
therefore always excellent and always different.
**Last night:** five blades, five bearers, one night of certification.
**History:** *tameshigiri*, sword rankings, official testers' certificates.
**Cost:** low, and it hands the enemy a natural equipment-variety engine.

### 21. Two Claimants
Two heirs, one domain, and a province that will burn if it comes to war. An old usage is invoked:
settle it with champions instead of armies. Schools are retained by both sides; the season is a
tally; the last night decides the succession.
**Grey/Black:** you are killing men to prevent a war, and telling yourself the arithmetic works ·
the other side's patron intends to win and then burn the province anyway.
**Clock:** the succession must be settled before the shogunate intervenes — and if it intervenes,
attainder takes the domain and everyone in it loses.
**Repetition:** the tally is the season.
**Last night:** five bouts, the succession.
**History:** invented on real ground — succession disputes provoked *kaieki*; loosened
inheritance rules were the state's post-Keian concession.
**Cost:** low-medium. Highest stakes, largest cast, most exposition.

### 22. The Body Trade
Your school's physician keeps it solvent by selling what the fights leave behind — corpses to the
testers, organs to the pill-makers. Your master found out and was killed by his own doctor's
buyer.
**Grey/Black:** the school has been funded by its own dead for years and everyone half knew ·
the buyer needs a steady supply and has stopped waiting for natural causes.
**Clock:** the physician's licence and the school's are renewed together.
**Repetition:** every fight feeds the ledger you are trying to destroy.
**Last night:** five men who came for the bodies.
**History:** unclaimed corpses handed to testers; organs rendered into commercial medicines.
**Cost:** low. The darkest of the set, and the most specific.

### 23. The Ship
A last legal sailing before the sea closes. Get your people aboard and they live; miss it and
they are conscripted, or worse. The permit is issued to one school in the province.
**Grey/Black:** you are fighting other desperate schools for a lifeboat that fits forty · the
official selling the permit auctions it every year and has no intention of honouring it.
**Clock:** the sailing date. The hardest possible deadline.
**Repetition:** the permit is bid for in fights, not money.
**Last night:** five schools' champions at the quay.
**History:** red-seal permits; the 1635 ban on overseas travel and return.
**Cost:** low.

### 24. The Hostage Yard
An older setting, the wars not yet finished. Houses exchange hostages as surety; the hostages are
children, and someone must feed, house and train them. That is your school. If the truce breaks,
the law of hostages says they die.
**Grey/Black:** you are training children who may have to be killed, and you take the fee · the
house planning to break the truce has already sold your yard's location.
**Clock:** the truce's term.
**Repetition:** raids to take hostages back, duels over insults that would break the truce.
**Last night:** five attempts on the yard in one night, as the truce ends.
**History:** hostage exchange was standard Sengoku practice.
**Cost:** medium — moves the period back, invalidates the peacetime framing.

### 25. The Widow's Name
The school passes to your master's widow, who cannot legally hold the licence. You hold it for
her; she runs it. The rival school's suit argues the licence is void — and if the suit succeeds,
the students and their contracts transfer with it.
**Grey/Black:** the school survives on a legal fiction and everyone in it lies routinely · the
rival is exploiting a law that is not of his making.
**Clock:** the suit is heard at the end of the season.
**Repetition:** each victory is evidence the school is real.
**Last night:** the court's demonstration — five matches to prove the school teaches something.
**History:** licensing and succession within *ryūha*; women's exclusion from formal headship.
**Cost:** low.

### 26. The Man Who Cut the Master
Simplest of all: a name, a face, a school. Your master lost a match he should have won, because
his opponent's students had trained on living people. That school's head is now the province's
most respected teacher.
**Grey/Black:** you will do anything to reach him, including things his school does · he built
excellence on capital crimes and it worked.
**Clock:** he accepts one formal match a year, at the season's end, and only from a school of
standing.
**Repetition:** standing is built fight by fight; the ladder is the season.
**Last night:** his four senior students, then him.
**History:** *tsujigiri* as capital offence 1602; *taryū jiai*.
**Cost:** none. The cleanest emotional line and the least new writing.

### 27. The Pardon
Your people are pardoned criminals — sentences commuted into service. The pardon is conditional
and reviewed. The magistrate who signs the review is paid, by someone, to revoke.
**Grey/Black:** you hold their pardons and therefore own them · the man buying revocations
resells the revoked into the mine.
**Clock:** the review.
**Repetition:** each fight is a line in the record that argues for a man.
**Last night:** five pardons, read out one by one, each contested by a fight.
**History:** commutation into service; *mushukunin* roundups; the mine's ten-year terms.
**Cost:** none.

### 28. The Fire Season
The city burns every few years, and rebuilding is the biggest business there is. Labour gangs
fight over reconstruction contracts; the fighting is licensed because the alternative is riots.
Your school holds a crew.
**Grey/Black:** you fight over other people's disasters for wages · the boss opposite you is
setting the fires.
**Clock:** the rebuilding contracts are let for the year at season's end.
**Repetition:** contract by contract, district by district.
**Last night:** five crews, one night, the last district.
**History:** Edo's fires and firefighting bands; machi-yakko street organisation. (Verify the
specific fire before dating it.)
**Cost:** low, but the setting drifts from a fighting school to a labour war.

### 29. The Boy Who Was Sixteen
A charismatic boy is gathering the desperate again, twenty years after the last rising ended with
thirty-seven thousand dead. He is not wrong about anything. He will still get them all killed,
and your students are listening to him.
**Grey/Black:** you side with the state you despise, to keep forty people alive · the boy is
sympathetic and the officer suppressing him is right.
**Clock:** the rising is planned for a date everyone knows.
**Repetition:** each fight pulls specific men back from him.
**Last night:** five of your own former students, at the gate, on the night it begins.
**History:** Shimabara and Amakusa Shirō; the Keian plot of 1651 and its thirty-three executions.
**Cost:** low-medium. The hardest to write and the best ending available — you win by having
fewer people die.

### 30. The Name Is the Prize
No villain at the centre at all. The province keeps a register of schools, and at the end of the
season it is rewritten: one name at the top, and every name below it dependent on that one for
students, patrons and licences. Everyone in the province is fighting for a line of text. Your
master died three places from the top and left you his students and his ranking.
**Grey/Black:** the system consumes people and you are competing in it enthusiastically · the
school above you is doing exactly what you are doing, slightly better.
**Clock:** the register is rewritten annually.
**Repetition:** the ladder.
**Last night:** the top five, in order.
**History:** school rankings, licensing, patronage economics.
**Cost:** none — this is the game as currently built, with the fiction removed rather than added.
Worth keeping on the list as the honest minimum.

## Reading the list

**If the aim is the least new work:** 1, 6, 26, 30.
**If the aim is the strongest villain:** 2, 13, 9 — none of whom break a law.
**If the aim is the most original:** 12, 17, 22.
**If the aim is the best fit for the chat layer:** 15 — public sentiment *is* the audience — and
3, where the crowd's power is denunciation.
**If the aim is the strongest ending:** 29, where winning means fewer of your people die.

The concepts combine. 1 supplies the moral spine, 13 supplies the clock, 26 supplies the face,
and 2 supplies the buyer. That combination needs no new systems at all.

## Appendix — the official-post precedent (verified 2026-09-10)

Added while settling the story's clock. The chosen premise — an official post whose holder
licenses the schools, left vacant, filled by a contest in the lord's presence, with a patron
working the appointment from behind — is not invented in outline. Its documented case is the
game of Go, not swordsmanship:

- **Godokoro** — the shogunate's appointed head of the discipline. Its duties included approving
  rank promotions, mediating disputes between the four houses and tutoring the shogun. The first
  holder, Hon'inbo Sansa, was appointed by Tokugawa Ieyasu himself.
- **Oshirogo** — the four houses played before the shogun at Edo Castle on a fixed date each
  year, the 17th day of the 11th month.
- **Sogo / arasoi-go ("disputed games")** — appointments to Meijin-godokoro were settled by
  challenge matches; the first was fought between the second Hon'inbo head and the second head
  of the Yasui house.
- **The patron route** — Yasui Sanchi became Meijin-godokoro in 1668 "by a backstairs route,
  with influence exerted by the head of the Matsudaira clan", which was later contested in a
  twenty-game match against Hon'inbo Doetsu (1668-1675).

**What was not found:** any case of a domain's sword-instructor post (*shinan-yaku*) being filled
by a contest. Applying the Go system to a sword school is our transposition, and `STORY.md` marks
it as invented.

**A correction, also from this pass:** the six-month *sankin-kotai* alternation is real but dates
from **1684** and applied only to the Kanto *fudai* lords. It cannot be used to justify a 180-day
absence in the 1630s-40s. The story therefore treats the 180 days as the notice period published
for the appointment, not as a rotation cycle.
