1

No primary decision. Three panels of equal weight, equal borders, equal density. The screen's whole job is one choice — take this contract or spend the day another way — and nothing on it is sized like that choice.

2

The party list is not a judgement. It says who is fit; it never says whether these three beat what is waiting. The player has to hold the threat band in their head while reading a second column.

3

The field is written, not felt. "Mud, dawn fog" plus a sentence of rules. Which of my men does that hurt, and by how much? The answer exists in the core and is never shown.

4

Buttons with no consequence. Three stacked buttons of the same size. Taking the bounty spends two days, 18 food and 12 water, and returns on day 25 — none of that is attached to the button that does it.

5

The log eats a third of the screen to report things that already happened and that you cannot act on. Its useful part is two or three lines: what changed since yesterday.

6

Eight numbers, no thresholds. The store row treats gold, sake and the nearly-empty medicine chest as equals. Only one of them should be shouting today.

7

Season pressure is scattered. The clock is top right, heads taken is in the store row, the summons is bottom right, standing is in two places. These are one thing: how the campaign is going and what is closing in.

8

Two readings of honour. Standing appears as a store chip and again as a gauge with a paragraph. One of them is enough.

What holds up: the information architecture is right — offer, party, dojo, season. Nothing needs removing from the game, only re-ranking on the screen. The fix is hierarchy and consequence, not new systems.
